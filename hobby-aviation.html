1949

Alma Tharp and The Ninety-Nines — Carolina Women Pilots and Air Race, 1949

May 21, 1949
Charleston, SC
Alma B

Collection of May 1949 newspaper clippings documenting Alma Tharp's aviation activities and involvement with the Carolinas Chapter of The Ninety-Nines, the international organization of licensed women pilots. A May 21, 1949 News and Courier article records Alma receiving her private pilot license at Carolina Skyways Airport. That same weekend, Alma flew her trim Ercoupe from Charleston to Spartanburg for the Carolinas Chapter's annual meeting. Other clippings identify her alongside fellow women pilots including Jewell Bailey. The chapter meetings included planning for the upcoming All-Woman Transcontinental Air Race, scheduled to begin in San Diego on June 11 and finish in Greenville, South Carolina. Charleston was selected as the race's eastern continental marker, requiring competitors to fly through Charleston before proceeding to Greenville. Dates represented: May 21 and May 25, 1949.

Ninety-Nines Women Pilots, 1950

May 31, 1950
Charleston, SC
Alma B

Newspaper clippings and a Carolinas Chapter newsletter document Alma Tharp of Folly Beach, South Carolina, as an active member of The Ninety-Nines, the international organization of licensed women pilots. In 1950, Alma was described as the youngest member of the Southeastern section of the two Carolinas. She and fellow Charleston pilot Jewell Bailey represented the organization at aviation events and conventions, including the Southeastern convention in Brunswick, Georgia, and had previously served as hostesses at the national Ninety-Nines convention in Greenville. The Carolinas Chapter newsletter also identifies Alma as a “99er from Charleston” and reports that she won a prize at an S.C. Breakfast Club meeting at Newberry for flying the greatest distance to attend. Together, the items document Alma's active participation in South Carolina aviation and the organized community of women pilots during 1950. Dates visible on the archive page: May 31, 1950 and September 5, 1950.

1956

Lancaster Girl Pilot Proves Woman’s Place Is in Airplane — Alma Bell and Her Ercoupe

Circa August 1956
Lancaster County, SC
Alma B

The Lancaster News profile of Alma Edythe Tharp Bell (Mrs. Buster Bell) of Lancaster, South Carolina, describing her extensive involvement in aviation. The article reports that Alma had been flying since age 14, had accumulated approximately 500 flying hours, and at age 24 was teaching her husband, James Stewart “Buster” Bell Jr., to fly in preparation for his pilot’s license. Alma owned an Ercoupe two-seat airplane and was the only Lancaster woman known to belong to The Ninety-Nines, the international organization of licensed women pilots. She had been a member for five years and also belonged to the Civil Air Patrol and the South Carolina Breakfast Club. The article recalls that Alma's father initially objected to her interest in flying but later took lessons himself, obtained his pilot's license, and bought an airplane. At the time of the article, Alma and Buster were living at 407 East Gay Street in Lancaster. Alma worked in the order department of Springs Cotton Mills and continued flying with Buster on weekends.

1968

Marine Major John J. Tharp Returns Home After Being Wounded in Vietnam — March 8, 1968

March 8, 1968
Johnny Tharp

Globe newspaper article dated Friday, March 8, 1968, documenting the return of Marine Major John J. Tharp after he was wounded while serving as a helicopter pilot in Vietnam. Major Tharp had deployed to Vietnam in March 1967 and served with Marine Medium Helicopter Squadron HMM-165 aboard the USS Valley Forge. During a mission supporting Marines at the Huế Citadel, Tharp was piloting a CH-46 helicopter when enemy fire struck the aircraft and a round ricocheted into his leg. Despite being wounded, he turned the controls over to his copilot and was flown to Phu Bai, then evacuated through Cam Ranh Bay and Japan for further treatment. Major Tharp returned to the United States on February 28, 1968, approximately three weeks after being wounded. The accompanying photograph shows his reunion with his wife, Mrs. John J. Tharp, and their children, John Jr., age 7, and Lori, at the Marine Corps Air Facility, New River, North Carolina. The article recounts that before leaving for Vietnam, Tharp had reassured his family that he would return. His son John Jr. reportedly greeted him by saying, “No, Daddy, we just knew you would come home.”

1994

Alma Tharp Bell – A Lifetime of Aviation and a Record-Setting Legacy

May 19, 1994
Lancaster County, SC
Alma B

Retrospective newspaper article by J. Douglas Donehue recounting the aviation career of Alma Edythe Tharp Bell. Alma made her first solo flight at age 16 and became one of the relatively few licensed women pilots active in South Carolina during the late 1940s and early 1950s. She frequently flew with fellow aviator and close friend Jewell Bailey in Alma's airplane, named “Rebel” after Charleston's South Atlantic League baseball team. The article describes Alma and Jewell's involvement with The Ninety-Nines, the organization founded by Amelia Earhart and other women pilots. In 1949, Jewell Bailey set a light-airplane altitude record at the Azalea Festival, flying a Piper Cub to 26,875 feet, 1,112 feet above the previous record. In 1947, Alma flew her father, Graham Tharp, to Atlanta to see a new Ercoupe. He liked the airplane so much that he purchased it for Alma. Alma later taught her father to fly and also taught her 14-year-old brother Johnny Tharp. In 1950, she served as an official hostess for the national Ninety-Nines convention in Greenville and was described as the organization's youngest member. As Alma's reputation grew, she and Jewell Bailey became well known in regional aviation circles, flying to air shows throughout the Carolinas and Southeast. Alma grew up at Folly Beach, later married James Stewart “Buster” Bell Jr. in 1952, and moved to Lancaster. The article states that Alma was 67 years old when she died on May 19.

The First Solo – Cutting the Shirt Tail

April 3, 2023
Confederate Ave, Lancaster County, SC
Rickey B

One of aviation’s oldest traditions happens after a student pilot completes their first solo flight. In the early days of flying, instructors often sat behind their students in tandem aircraft. With no radios or intercoms, the instructor would sometimes get the student’s attention by tugging on the back of their shirt. Once the student successfully flew alone, they no longer needed the instructor pulling on their shirttail. So after that first solo, the instructor cuts the tail off the student pilot’s shirt. The shirttail is usually dated and marked with the aircraft, airport, or other details from the flight and kept as a reminder of the accomplishment. For a pilot, that cut shirt represents something much bigger than an old piece of fabric—it marks the first time you took an airplane up, flew it, and brought it safely back down completely on your own. Your first solo is a flight you never forget.

Cedar Key, Florida – Fly-In Adventure

Flying into Cedar Key, Florida is an adventure all by itself. The small island airport sits right along the Gulf, giving you beautiful views of the water and islands as you come in to land.
