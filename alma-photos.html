Alma Tharp Joins Memminger High School Victory Corps — World War II Community Service

March 9, 1943
Charleston, SC
Alma B

Newspaper article announcing the formation of a Victory Corps unit at Memminger High School in Charleston, South Carolina, as part of the school's contribution to the World War II home-front effort. The program was designed to train high-school students for future war service while encouraging active participation in community wartime activities. Students were required to meet academic, physical-training and community-service standards. Activities included Red Cross work, Civilian Defense assistance, USO support, rationing-board work, selling war bonds and stamps, victory gardens, and other wartime service projects. Among the students listed as enrolling in the Victory Corps is Alma Thorp [Tharp], who attended Memminger High School before becoming known for her aviation activities and membership in The Ninety-Nines.

Alma Tharp Serves in Memminger Victory Corps Nurses Aides Squad

Circa May 1943
Charleston, SC
Alma B

Newspaper article reporting the organization and officers of the Memminger Victory Corps at Memminger High School in Charleston, South Carolina. 1949

Alma Tharp and The Ninety-Nines — Carolina Women Pilots and Air Race, 1949

May 21, 1949
Charleston, SC
Alma B

Collection of May 1949 newspaper clippings documenting Alma Tharp's aviation activities and involvement with the Carolinas Chapter of The Ninety-Nines, the international organization of licensed women pilots. A May 21, 1949 News and Courier article records Alma receiving her private pilot license at Carolina Skyways Airport. That same weekend, Alma flew her trim Ercoupe from Charleston to Spartanburg for the Carolinas Chapter's annual meeting. Other clippings identify her alongside fellow women pilots including Jewell Bailey, Kathleen O'Brien, Amalie Stone, Anne Fishburne, Vera Self, and Louise Smith. The chapter meetings included planning for the upcoming All-Woman Transcontinental Air Race, scheduled to begin in San Diego on June 11 and finish in Greenville, South Carolina. Charleston was selected as the race's eastern continental marker, requiring competitors to fly through Charleston before proceeding to Greenville. The clippings also document the rapid growth of The Ninety-Nines, founded in 1929 with Amelia Earhart as its first president, and Alma's participation in the organization during an important period in the development of women's civilian aviation. Dates represented: May 21 and May 25, 1949.

Ninety-Nines Women Pilots, 1950

May 31, 1950
Charleston, SC
Alma B

Newspaper clippings and a Carolinas Chapter newsletter document Alma Tharp of Folly Beach, South Carolina, as an active member of The Ninety-Nines, the international organization of licensed women pilots. In 1950, Alma was described as the youngest member of the Southeastern section of the two Carolinas. She and fellow Charleston pilot Jewell Bailey represented the organization at aviation events and conventions, including the Southeastern convention in Brunswick, Georgia, and had previously served as hostesses at the national Ninety-Nines convention in Greenville. The Carolinas Chapter newsletter also identifies Alma as a “99er from Charleston” and reports that she won a prize at an S.C. Breakfast Club meeting at Newberry for flying the greatest distance to attend. Together, the items document Alma's active participation in South Carolina aviation and the organized community of women pilots during 1950. Dates visible on the archive page: May 31, 1950 and September 5, 1950.

1956

Lancaster Girl Pilot Proves Woman’s Place Is in Airplane — Alma Bell and Her Ercoupe

Circa August 1956
Lancaster County, SC
Alma B

The Lancaster News profile of Alma Edythe Tharp Bell (Mrs. Buster Bell) of Lancaster, South Carolina, describing her extensive involvement in aviation. The article reports that Alma had been flying since age 14, had accumulated approximately 500 flying hours, and at age 24 was teaching her husband, James Stewart “Buster” Bell Jr., to fly in preparation for his pilot’s license. Alma owned an Ercoupe two-seat airplane and was the only Lancaster woman known to belong to The Ninety-Nines, the international organization of licensed women pilots. She had been a member for five years and also belonged to the Civil Air Patrol and the South Carolina Breakfast Club. The article recalls that Alma's father initially objected to her interest in flying but later took lessons himself, obtained his pilot's license, and bought an airplane. The story also recounts several of Alma's aviation adventures, including an Ercoupe landing-gear problem on a return flight from Charleston, an engine failure that forced a dead-stick landing, and a flight to Batesburg during which heavy ground fog caused her to become lost before she navigated home from Spartanburg. At the time of the article, Alma and Buster were living at 407 East Gay Street in Lancaster. Alma worked in the order department of Springs Cotton Mills and continued flying with Buster on weekends.

Alma Tharp Turned Love of Flying Into Daring Hobby and Set Record

May 19, 1994
Lancaster County, SC
Alma B

Retrospective newspaper article by J. Douglas Donehue recounting the aviation career of Alma Edythe Tharp Bell. Alma made her first solo flight at age 16 and became one of the relatively few licensed women pilots active in South Carolina during the late 1940s and early 1950s. She frequently flew with fellow aviator and close friend Jewell Bailey in Alma's airplane, named “Rebel” after Charleston's South Atlantic League baseball team. The article describes Alma and Jewell's involvement with The Ninety-Nines, the organization founded by Amelia Earhart and other women pilots. In 1949, Jewell Bailey set a light-airplane altitude record at the Azalea Festival, flying a Piper Cub to 26,875 feet, 1,112 feet above the previous record. In 1947, Alma flew her father, Graham Tharp, to Atlanta to see a new Ercoupe. He liked the airplane so much that he purchased it for Alma. Alma later taught her father to fly and also taught her 14-year-old brother Johnny Tharp. In 1950, she served as an official hostess for the national Ninety-Nines convention in Greenville and was described as the organization's youngest member. As Alma's reputation grew, she and Jewell Bailey became well known in regional aviation circles, flying to air shows throughout the Carolinas and Southeast. Alma grew up at Folly Beach, later married James Stewart “Buster” Bell Jr. in 1952, and moved to Lancaster. The article states that Alma was 67 years old when she died on May 19.
